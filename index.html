Why people come to see me

Life is complicated, contradictory and sometimes difficult to make sense of. Most of us are trying to work out how to live it as we go, and sometimes it becomes too much to carry by ourselves.

You might be dealing with something quite clear — anxiety, depression, trauma, a relationship, loneliness, identity, or something that has happened in your life. Or you might simply find that you are not living in the way you want to be, without really knowing why.

You do not need a diagnosis or a neat explanation before coming to therapy. It can be somewhere to talk honestly, understand yourself and your life more clearly, and have another person alongside you while you work out what you want and what might need to change.

Wherever you are, I will meet you as another human being, and we can begin to make sense of things together.

About me

Depth, warmth,
and genuine presence

I am a psychotherapist based in Canterbury, working in person from a plant-filled conservatory in a home-based setting, and online.

Before training as a psychotherapist, my academic background was in mathematics and physics, which I studied at UCL in London. That background has shaped the way I think and allows me to work comfortably with both scientific thinking and the experiential and philosophical perspectives at the heart of psychotherapy, which I see as both an art and a science.

I am curious and philosophical by nature, and bring that curiosity and a sense of playfulness to my work while not shying away from the darker, more difficult or contradictory parts of being human.

When we meet, I’m less interested in putting a label on what is happening than in understanding you as a person and what your life is actually like. If we agree to work together, you can expect an authentic, collaborative relationship focused on what matters most to you. I think people need connection, and therapy can offer somewhere to think and explore with another person rather than carrying everything alone.

Neurodiversity and LGBTQ+ communities are close to my heart, as are questions around gender, equality and justice. I work openly and affirmatively, while remaining curious about each person’s own experience, culture and identity.

Outside work, I enjoy meditation, yoga, martial arts and getting out on the water on my paddleboard; much of this also influences how I practise.

Alongside my clinical practice, I am undertaking doctoral research exploring the lived experience of fatherhood. I am also passionate about working with parents and exploring the experience and challenges of parenthood, regardless of gender.

How I work

Rooted in depth.
Open to what helps.

My core training is in psychodynamic and existential psychotherapy. We may explore how your past and relationships have shaped you, but also how you are living now, what matters to you, the meaning you make of your life, and how you want to live. This is often longer-term, in-depth work, but we also pay attention to what is difficult in the present and what may help bring some relief now. I try to understand the whole of your experience — personal, social, physical and spiritual — and draw on other approaches where they are useful. I follow the person, not the method.

Psychodynamic

Psychodynamic therapy pays attention to attachment, recurring patterns, anxieties, and the ways we have learned to relate to and protect ourselves. We explore how these developed and how they may still be influencing your relationships and experience in the present.

Insight creates room to change.

Existential

Existential therapy draws on centuries of philosophical thinking about what it means to be human. We explore your experience across all dimensions of life, not just the psychological, but also the embodied, social and spiritual, alongside questions of meaning, freedom, responsibility and choice.

Find your truth; live in a way that feels meaningful and fulfilling to you.

Integrative

I draw on other approaches where they are useful, including CBT, mindfulness, relational and family systems ideas. This might include homework, inner child work, or support with communication, boundaries and assertiveness. I work in a trauma-informed way throughout, combining practical help with deeper work.

Therapy built around your needs.

Common questions

Things people
often ask

What happens in the first session?+
The first session is mostly about me understanding what's brought you to therapy and beginning to get a sense of what you need. I'll ask some questions, but there's no pressure to have everything worked out. Many people find it helpful just to say things out loud for the first time.
Do I need to know what's wrong before I get in touch?+
Not at all. Many people contact me with little more than a sense that something isn't right. You don't need a diagnosis, a clear problem, or the right words. That's partly what therapy is for: working out what's actually going on.
What if I get emotional, or don't know what to say?+
That's completely okay. You're welcome to bring all of you, including silence, tears, uncertainty, or not knowing where to begin. I won't rush you, and if something feels too much, we can pause.
How do I know if you're the right therapist for me?+
A good fit usually means feeling safe enough to speak openly, even if therapy still feels challenging. You don't need to know immediately. We can use the first session to get a sense of whether working together feels right. If I'm not the right person, I'll do my best to point you in the right direction.
What if I've tried therapy before and it didn't help?+
That can feel disheartening, but it doesn't mean therapy cannot help. You haven't had this therapy, with me, at this point in your life. Sometimes it takes the right therapist, the right timing, or a different kind of relationship for something to shift.
How long does therapy take?+
It varies. Some people come for a short period to work through something specific. Others choose longer-term therapy to explore deeper patterns, relationships, or questions about who they are and how they want to live. We can review the work as we go.
Is online therapy as effective as in-person?+
Online therapy can work very well for many people. Some prefer the convenience and familiarity of being in their own space, while others value meeting in person. Neither is automatically better. They are different, and we can discuss what feels right for you.
Is everything I say confidential?+
Yes. What you share is confidential, with standard ethical and safeguarding exceptions, such as serious risk of harm to you or someone else. I take confidentiality seriously and will explain it clearly before we begin, so you know where you stand.
